Chapter 01

Fundamentals & The Power Rule

At its heart, a derivative measures how fast a function is changing at one exact instant. Visually, if you zoom in closely on a smooth curve at \(x = x_0\), the curve looks like a straight line—the derivative \(f'(x_0)\) is simply the slope of that tangent line.

Core Rules Box
1. Constant Rule
$$\frac{d}{dx}[c] = 0$$

A standalone constant never changes, so its slope is zero.

2. Power Rule
$$\frac{d}{dx}[x^n] = n \cdot x^{n-1}$$

Bring the exponent down in front, then subtract \(1\) from it.

Algebraic Rewrite Rule: The Power Rule only works when \(x\) is sitting in the numerator with an exponent. Before differentiating fractions or roots, rewrite them using negative or fractional exponents:

$$\frac{1}{x^n} = x^{-n} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \sqrt[n]{x^m} = x^{m/n}$$
Worked Example 1.1

Find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = 4x^3 - \frac{2}{x} + 3\sqrt{x} - 9\)

Step 1 — Rewrite every term with clean exponents

Convert \(\frac{2}{x}\) into \(2x^{-1}\) and \(\sqrt{x}\) into \(x^{1/2}\):

$$f(x) = 4x^3 - 2x^{-1} + 3x^{1/2} - 9$$
Step 2 — Apply the Power Rule term by term

Multiply each coefficient by its exponent and subtract \(1\) from the power (the constant \(-9\) becomes \(0\)):

$$f'(x) = 4(3x^2) - 2(-1x^{-2}) + 3\left(\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\right) - 0$$
Step 3 — Final Simplified Answer
$$f'(x) = 12x^2 + 2x^{-2} + \frac{3}{2}x^{-1/2} = 12x^2 + \frac{2}{x^2} + \frac{3}{2\sqrt{x}}$$
Chapter 02

Product & Quotient Rules

A classic exam mistake is assuming that the derivative of \(u \cdot v\) is simply \(u' \cdot v'\). That is false! When two expressions involving \(x\) are multiplied or divided, use the Product and Quotient Rules below. To keep things crystal clear, we highlight u (First / Top) in blue and v (Second / Bottom) in amber.

Product Rule
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\color{#0284c7}{u} \cdot \color{#d97706}{v}\color{black}{]} = \color{#0284c7}{u}\color{black}{ \cdot }\color{#d97706}{v'}\color{black}{ + }\color{#d97706}{v}\color{black}{ \cdot }\color{#0284c7}{u'}$$

First times derivative of Second, plus Second times derivative of First.

Quotient Rule
$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\color{#0284c7}{u}}{\color{#d97706}{v}}\right] = \frac{\color{#d97706}{v}\color{black}{ \cdot }\color{#0284c7}{u'}\color{black}{ - }\color{#0284c7}{u}\color{black}{ \cdot }\color{#d97706}{v'}}{\color{#d97706}{v^2}}$$

Order matters because of the minus sign! Always start with bottom (\(v\)).

Worked Example 2.1 (Product Rule)

Differentiate \(g(x) = (x^2 + 3)(2x - 5)\)

Step 1 — Label \(u\) and \(v\) and find their derivatives

Let \(u = x^2 + 3 \implies u' = 2x\) and \(v = 2x - 5 \implies v' = 2\).

Step 2 — Substitute into \(u \cdot v' + v \cdot u'\)
$$g'(x) = (x^2 + 3)(2) + (2x - 5)(2x)$$
Step 3 — Expand and combine like terms
$$g'(x) = (2x^2 + 6) + (4x^2 - 10x) = 6x^2 - 10x + 6$$
Worked Example 2.2 (Quotient Rule)

Differentiate \(h(x) = \frac{3x + 1}{x^2 - 4}\)

Step 1 — Identify Top (\(u\)) and Bottom (\(v\))

Top: \(u = 3x + 1 \implies u' = 3\)  |  Bottom: \(v = x^2 - 4 \implies v' = 2x\).

Step 2 — Plug into \(\frac{v \cdot u' - u \cdot v'}{v^2}\)
$$h'(x) = \frac{(x^2 - 4)(3) - (3x + 1)(2x)}{(x^2 - 4)^2}$$
Step 3 — Simplify the numerator (leave bottom factored)
$$h'(x) = \frac{3x^2 - 12 - (6x^2 + 2x)}{(x^2 - 4)^2} = \frac{-3x^2 - 2x - 12}{(x^2 - 4)^2}$$
Chapter 03

The Chain Rule (Composite Functions)

Whenever a function is nested inside another function—like \((5x^2 + 1)^4\) or \(\sin(3x)\)—think of peeling an onion from the outside in. Differentiate the outer shell first while keeping the inside expression \(u\) unchanged, then multiply by the derivative of the inside (\(u'\)).

General Power & Chain Rule
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[u^n] = n \cdot u^{n-1} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}$$

Chapter 05

Exponential & Natural Log Derivatives

Exponential functions and natural logarithms appear constantly on engineering exams. Keep these three formulas side-by-side:

1. Natural Base \(e^u\)
$$\frac{d}{dx}[e^u] = e^u \cdot \frac{du}{dx}$$

Copy \(e^u\) exactly as it is, then multiply by the derivative of the exponent.

2. Any Base \(a^u\) (\(a > 0\))
$$\frac{d}{dx}[a^u] = a^u \ln(a) \cdot \frac{du}{dx}$$

Same as \(e^u\), except you attach \(\ln(a)\) because the base is \(a\) instead of \(e\).

3. Natural Log \(\ln(u)\)
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\ln u] = \frac{1}{u} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} = \frac{u'}{u}$$

Put the inside \(u\) in the denominator and its derivative \(u'\) on top.

Worked Example 5.1

Differentiate \(f(x) = e^{\sin(2x)} + \ln(x^3 + 4)\)

Step 1 — Differentiate the first term \(e^{\sin(2x)}\)

Copy \(e^{\sin(2x)}\), then multiply by \(\frac{d}{dx}[\sin(2x)] = 2\cos(2x)\):

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[e^{\sin(2x)}\right] = 2\cos(2x) \cdot e^{\sin(2x)}$$
Step 2 — Differentiate the second term \(\ln(x^3 + 4)\)

Using \(\frac{u'}{u}\) with \(u = x^3 + 4\) and \(u' = 3x^2\):

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\ln(x^3 + 4)\right] = \frac{3x^2}{x^3 + 4}$$
Step 3 — Combine both results
$$f'(x) = 2\cos(2x)\,e^{\sin(2x)} + \frac{3x^2}{x^3 + 4}$$
Chapter 06

Implicit Differentiation & Higher-Order Derivatives

Part A: Higher-Order Derivatives

Finding the second derivative \(f''(x)\) (or \(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\)) simply means differentiating \(f'(x)\) one more time. For example, if \(f(x) = x^4\), then \(f'(x) = 4x^3\), \(f''(x) = 12x^2\), and \(f'''(x) = 24x\).

Part B: Implicit Differentiation

Sometimes an equation mixes \(x\) and \(y\) together (such as a circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\)) where solving for \(y\) explicitly is messy or impossible. In Implicit Differentiation, we differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\) directly—remembering that \(y\) is a hidden function of \(x\):

Golden Implicit Rule: Differentiate \(x\)-terms normally. Whenever you differentiate a \(y\)-term, multiply by \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) (or \(y'\)) by the Chain Rule. For example: \(\frac{d}{dx}[y^3] = 3y^2 \frac{dy}{dx}\).
Worked Example 6.1

Find \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) if \(x^2 + y^3 = 4y + 10\)

Step 1 — Differentiate every term with respect to \(x\)

Attach \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) whenever differentiating \(y^3\) and \(4y\):

$$2x + 3y^2\frac{dy}{dx} = 4\frac{dy}{dx} + 0$$
Step 2 — Group all \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) terms on the left side
$$3y^2\frac{dy}{dx} - 4\frac{dy}{dx} = -2x$$
Step 3 — Factor out \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) and divide
$$\frac{dy}{dx}(3y^2 - 4) = -2x \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-2x}{3y^2 - 4}$$

Definite Integrals & Riemann Sums

For a function \(f(x)\) defined on \([a, b]\), the definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) is defined as the limit of Riemann sums as the subinterval width \(\Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n} \to 0\). Geometrically, it represents the net signed area between \(y = f(x)\) and the \(x\)-axis (area above the axis minus area below).

Riemann Sum Definition & Summation Formulas
$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n \to \infty}\sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k^*)\,\Delta x, \qquad \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}, \quad x_k = a + k\Delta x$$
$$\sum_{k=1}^n k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$
$$\sum_{k=1}^n k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$$
$$\sum_{k=1}^n k^3 = \left[\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right]^2$$
Order & Zero-Width Rules
$$\int_a^a f(x)\,dx = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \int_b^a f(x)\,dx = -\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$$
Additivity & Comparison
$$\int_a^c f(x)\,dx + \int_c^b f(x)\,dx = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$$
Worked Example L1.1 (Riemann Limit)

Evaluate \(\int_0^2 (3x^2 + 1)\,dx\) using Right-Endpoint Riemann Sums

Step 1 — Compute \(\Delta x\) and right endpoints \(x_k\)
$$\Delta x = \frac{2 - 0}{n} = \frac{2}{n}, \qquad x_k = 0 + k\left(\frac{2}{n}\right) = \frac{2k}{n}$$
Step 2 — Form the Riemann sum \(R_n = \sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k)\Delta x\)
$$R_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \left[3\left(\frac{2k}{n}\right)^2 + 1\right]\frac{2}{n} = \frac{24}{n^3}\sum_{k=1}^n k^2 + \frac{2}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n 1 = \frac{24}{n^3}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} + 2$$
Step 3 — Take the limit as \(n \to \infty\)
$$\lim_{n \to \infty}\left[4\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)\left(2 + \frac{1}{n}\right) + 2\right] = 4(1)(2) + 2 = 10$$
Worked Example L1.2 (Geometric Area)

Evaluate \(\int_{-3}^3 \sqrt{9 - x^2}\,dx\) using Geometry

Step 1 — Identify the curve \(y = \sqrt{9 - x^2}\)

Squaring gives \(x^2 + y^2 = 9\) with \(y \ge 0\), which is the upper semicircle of radius \(r = 3\) centered at the origin.

Step 2 — Compute the semicircle area
$$\int_{-3}^3 \sqrt{9 - x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\pi r^2 = \frac{1}{2}\pi(3^2) = \frac{9\pi}{2}$$
Lecture 02 • Sections 5.4–5.6

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus & \(u\)-Substitution

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) links differentiation and integration as inverse processes—allowing us to evaluate definite integrals without taking Riemann limits, and to differentiate integral-defined functions directly.

FTC Part 1 (Derivative of an Integral)
$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt\right] = f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$$

If both limits vary, \(\frac{d}{dx}\int_{h(x)}^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt = f(g(x))g'(x) - f(h(x))h'(x)\).

FTC Part 2 (Evaluation Theorem)
$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a), \quad F'(x) = f(x)$$

In definite \(u\)-substitution, always convert limits: \(\int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\,du\).

Worked Example L2.1 (FTC Part 1 + Chain Rule)

Find \(F'(x)\) for \(F(x) = \int_{2x}^{x^3} \sqrt{t^2 + 5}\,dt\)

Step 1 — Split at a constant \(c = 0\) and flip the first integral
$$F(x) = -\int_0^{2x} \sqrt{t^2 + 5}\,dt + \int_0^{x^3} \sqrt{t^2 + 5}\,dt$$
Step 2 — Apply FTC Part 1 with Chain Rule to both terms
$$F'(x) = \sqrt{(x^3)^2 + 5}\cdot(3x^2) - \sqrt{(2x)^2 + 5}\cdot(2) = 3x^2\sqrt{x^6 + 5} - 2\sqrt{4x^2 + 5}$$
Worked Example L2.2 (Definite u-Substitution)

Evaluate \(\int_0^4 x\sqrt{x^2 + 9}\,dx\)

Step 1 — Choose \(u = x^2 + 9\) and update the limits of integration

Then \(du = 2x\,dx \implies x\,dx = \frac{1}{2}du\). When \(x = 0 \implies u = 9\); when \(x = 4 \implies u = 25\).

Step 2 — Integrate in \(u\) and evaluate between \(9\) and \(25\)
$$\frac{1}{2}\int_9^{25} u^{1/2}\,du = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}\right]_9^{25} = \frac{1}{3}(25^{3/2} - 9^{3/2}) = \frac{1}{3}(125 - 27) = \frac{98}{3}$$

Inverse Functions, Natural Log & General Exponentials

A function \(f\) has an inverse \(f^{-1}\) if and only if it is one-to-one (passes the Horizontal Line Test, guaranteed if \(f'(x) > 0\) or \(f'(x) < 0\) everywhere). Even without finding an explicit formula for \(f^{-1}\), we can compute its derivative at \(b = f(a)\) using the Inverse Function Derivative Theorem.

Inverse Derivative Rule
$$(f^{-1})'(b) = \frac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(b))} = \frac{1}{f'(a)}$$

Slope of \(f^{-1}\) at \((b, a)\) is the reciprocal of the slope of \(f\) at \((a, b)\).

Logarithmic Integration
$$\int \frac{1}{u}\,du = \ln|u| + C$$

Gives \(\int \tan x\,dx = \ln|\sec x| + C\) and \(\int \cot x\,dx = \ln|\sin x| + C\).

General Exponential Integral
$$\int a^u\,du = \frac{a^u}{\ln a} + C \quad (a > 0, a \neq 1)$$

Divide by \(\ln a\) when integrating base \(a\).

Worked Example L3.1 (Inverse Function Slope)

Let \(f(x) = x^3 + 2x + 1\). Find \((f^{-1})'(4)\) without solving for \(f^{-1}(x)\)

Step 1 — Find \(a = f^{-1}(4)\) by solving \(f(a) = 4\)

Since \(f'(x) = 3x^2 + 2 > 0\), \(f\) is strictly increasing. By inspection, \(f(1) = 1^3 + 2(1) + 1 = 4 \implies f^{-1}(4) = 1\).

Step 2 — Apply \((f^{-1})'(4) = \frac{1}{f'(1)}\)
$$f'(1) = 3(1)^2 + 2 = 5 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad (f^{-1})'(4) = \frac{1}{5}$$
Worked Example L3.2 (Logarithmic Differentiation)

Find \(y'\) for \(y = \frac{(x^2 + 1)^4 \sqrt{2x - 1}}{(3x + 5)^3}\)

Step 1 — Take \(\ln\) of both sides and expand using log laws
$$\ln y = 4\ln(x^2 + 1) + \frac{1}{2}\ln(2x - 1) - 3\ln(3x + 5)$$
Step 2 — Differentiate implicitly and multiply by \(y\)
$$\frac{y'}{y} = \frac{8x}{x^2 + 1} + \frac{1}{2x - 1} - \frac{9}{3x + 5} \implies y' = \frac{(x^2 + 1)^4\sqrt{2x - 1}}{(3x + 5)^3}\left[\frac{8x}{x^2 + 1} + \frac{1}{2x - 1} - \frac{9}{3x + 5}\right]$$
Lecture 04 • Sections 7.4–7.6

Exponential Growth/Decay & Inverse Trigonometric Functions

Whenever the rate of change of a quantity \(y(t)\) is proportional to its current amount (\(\frac{dy}{dt} = ky\)), the unique solution is the Exponential Growth/Decay Law \(y(t) = y_0 e^{kt}\). In the second half of Lecture 4, we restrict domains of trigonometric functions to define \(\arcsin x\), \(\arctan x\), and \(\operatorname{arcsec} x\) along with their derivatives and antiderivatives.

Inverse Trigonometric Derivatives & Integrals
Inverse Sine
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\arcsin u] = \frac{u'}{\sqrt{1 - u^2}}$$
$$\int \frac{du}{\sqrt{a^2 - u^2}} = \arcsin\left(\frac{u}{a}\right) + C$$
Inverse Tangent
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\arctan u] = \frac{u'}{1 + u^2}$$
$$\int \frac{du}{a^2 + u^2} = \frac{1}{a}\arctan\left(\frac{u}{a}\right) + C$$
Inverse Secant
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\operatorname{arcsec} u] = \frac{u'}{|u|\sqrt{u^2 - 1}}$$
$$\int \frac{du}{u\sqrt{u^2 - a^2}} = \frac{1}{a}\operatorname{arcsec}\left|\frac{u}{a}\right| + C$$
Worked Example L4.1 (Exponential Decay & Half-Life)

A radioactive sample decays from \(100\text{ mg}\) to \(50\text{ mg}\) in \(8\text{ hours}\). Find the mass after \(20\text{ hours}\)

Step 1 — Use half-life \(t = 8\) to find decay constant \(k\)
$$50 = 100e^{8k} \implies \frac{1}{2} = e^{8k} \implies k = -\frac{\ln 2}{8}$$
Step 2 — Evaluate \(y(20)\)
$$y(20) = 100e^{-\frac{20}{8}\ln 2} = 100(2)^{-2.5} = \frac{100}{4\sqrt{2}} = \frac{25}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 17.68\text{ mg}$$
Worked Example L4.2 (Inverse Tangent Integral)

Evaluate \(\int \frac{e^x}{9 + e^{2x}}\,dx\)

Step 1 — Match \(\int \frac{du}{a^2 + u^2}\) with \(a = 3\) and \(u = e^x\)

Since \(e^{2x} = (e^x)^2\), setting \(u = e^x\) gives \(du = e^x\,dx\).

Step 2 — Apply \(\frac{1}{a}\arctan\left(\frac{u}{a}\right) + C\)
$$\int \frac{du}{3^2 + u^2} = \frac{1}{3}\arctan\left(\frac{e^x}{3}\right) + C$$
Lecture 05 • Section 7.7

Hyperbolic & Inverse Hyperbolic Functions

Hyperbolic functions are even and odd exponential combinations that parameterize the unit hyperbola \(x^2 - y^2 = 1\) (just as \(\cos t, \sin t\) parameterize the unit circle). Notice a crucial sign difference: \(\frac{d}{dx}[\cosh x] = +\sinh x\) (no minus sign!), whereas \(\operatorname{sech} x, \operatorname{csch} x, \coth x\) all carry minus signs.

Definitions, Identities & Derivatives
Exponential Definitions
$$\sinh x = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2}$$
$$\cosh x = \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2}$$
Fundamental Identities
$$\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1$$
$$1 - \tanh^2 x = \operatorname{sech}^2 x$$
Key Derivatives
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\sinh u] = \cosh u \cdot u'$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\cosh u] = \sinh u \cdot u', \quad \frac{d}{dx}[\tanh u] = \operatorname{sech}^2 u \cdot u'$$
Worked Example L5.1 (Hyperbolic Chain Rule)

Differentiate \(y = \ln(\cosh(3x)) - \tanh(x^2)\)

Step 1 — Differentiate \(\ln(\cosh(3x))\) using \(\frac{u'}{u}\)
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\ln(\cosh(3x))] = \frac{3\sinh(3x)}{\cosh(3x)} = 3\tanh(3x)$$
Step 2 — Subtract \(\frac{d}{dx}[\tanh(x^2)] = 2x\operatorname{sech}^2(x^2)\)
$$y' = 3\tanh(3x) - 2x\operatorname{sech}^2(x^2)$$
Worked Example L5.2 (Inverse Hyperbolic Integration)

Evaluate \(\int_0^4 \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + 9}}\) using \(\sinh^{-1}\)

Step 1 — Use \(\int \frac{du}{\sqrt{u^2 + a^2}} = \sinh^{-1}\left(\frac{u}{a}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{u + \sqrt{u^2 + a^2}}{a}\right)\) with \(a = 3\)
$$\left[\sinh^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{3}\right)\right]_0^4 = \sinh^{-1}\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) - \sinh^{-1}(0)$$
Step 2 — Convert to exact logarithmic form
$$= \ln\left(\frac{4}{3} + \sqrt{\frac{16}{9} + 1}\right) - 0 = \ln\left(\frac{4}{3} + \frac{5}{3}\right) = \ln(3)$$

Separable Differential Equations

A first-order differential equation is called separable if \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) can be factored into a product of a pure \(x\)-function \(g(x)\) and a pure \(y\)-function \(h(y)\). To solve it, separate all \(y\)-terms onto the left with \(dy\) and all \(x\)-terms onto the right with \(dx\), then integrate both sides.

Separation of Variables Method
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = g(x)h(y) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy = g(x)\,dx \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \int \frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy = \int g(x)\,dx$$
Worked Example 7.1

Solve \(\frac{dy}{dx} = (1 + y)e^x, \quad y > -1\)

Step 1 — Separate variables \(y\) and \(x\)

Since \(y > -1\), \(1 + y \neq 0\). Multiply by \(dx\) and divide by \((1 + y)\):

$$\frac{dy}{1 + y} = e^x\,dx$$
Step 2 — Integrate both sides
$$\int \frac{dy}{1 + y} = \int e^x\,dx$$
Step 3 — Implicit Solution (combine constants into \(C\))
$$\ln(1 + y) = e^x + C$$
Worked Example 7.2

Solve \(y(x + 1)\frac{dy}{dx} = x(y^2 + 1), \quad x \neq -1\)

Step 1 — Write in differential form and separate
$$y(x + 1)\,dy = x(y^2 + 1)\,dx \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{y\,dy}{y^2 + 1} = \frac{x\,dx}{x + 1}$$
Step 2 — Use polynomial division \(\frac{x}{x + 1} = 1 - \frac{1}{x + 1}\) before integrating
$$\int \frac{y\,dy}{1 + y^2} = \int \left(1 - \frac{1}{x + 1}\right)dx$$
Step 3 — Complete both integrals
$$\frac{1}{2}\ln(1 + y^2) = x - \ln|x + 1| + C$$

L'Hôpital's Rule & All 7 Indeterminate Forms

When direct substitution in \(\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) yields \(\frac{0}{0}\) or \(\frac{\pm\infty}{\pm\infty}\), L'Hôpital's Rule states that you can differentiate the numerator and denominator separately (never use the Quotient Rule here!):

L'Hôpital's Rule (Only for \(\frac{0}{0}\) or \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\))
$$\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$$

Chapter 09 • Section 7.8

Relative Rates of Growth

When comparing how fast two positive functions \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) grow as \(x \to \infty\), we evaluate the limit of their ratio \(\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\):

Limit = 0
$$\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = 0$$

\(f(x)\) grows slower than \(g(x)\) (\(g\) dominates).

Limit = L (Finite, \(L \neq 0\))
$$\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = L$$

\(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) grow at the same rate.

Limit = \(\infty\)
$$\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \infty$$

\(f(x)\) grows faster than \(g(x)\) (\(f\) dominates).

Chapter 10 • Section 8.2

Integration by Parts & Tabular Integration

Critical Warning: Integration is NOT distributive over multiplication or division! $$\int f(x) \cdot g(x)\,dx \;\neq\; \int f(x)\,dx \cdot \int g(x)\,dx$$

Derived directly from the Product Rule \(d(uv) = u\,dv + v\,du\), Integration by Parts rewrites a difficult product integral into an easier one:

Integration by Parts Formula
$$\int \color{#0284c7}{u}\,\color{#d97706}{dv}\color{black}{ = }\color{#0284c7}{u}\color{#d97706}{v}\color{black}{ - \int }\color{#d97706}{v}\,\color{#0284c7}{du}$$

Rules of Thumb: (1) Take the polynomial as \(u\) (so its derivative gets simpler), provided the other factor \(dv\) is easily integrable. (2) If applying IBP twice, never exchange your choice of \(u\) and \(dv\) mid-way!

Worked Example 10.1

Evaluate \(\int x \cos x\,dx\)

Step 1 — Choose polynomial \(u = x\) and \(dv = \cos x\,dx\)

Differentiate \(u\) and integrate \(dv\):

$$u = x \implies du = dx \qquad \text{and} \qquad dv = \cos x\,dx \implies v = \sin x$$
Step 2 — Substitute into \(uv - \int v\,du\)
$$\int x \cos x\,dx = x \sin x - \int \sin x\,dx$$
Step 3 — Evaluate remaining integral (remember \(\int \sin x\,dx = -\cos x\))
$$\int x \cos x\,dx = x \sin x + \cos x + C$$
Worked Example 10.2 • Tabular Integration Shortcut

Evaluate \(\int x^2 e^x\,dx\) without repeating IBP twice

List \(f(x) = x^2\) and its derivatives on the left until reaching \(0\), and \(g(x) = e^x\) and its antiderivatives on the right. Multiply diagonally with alternating signs \((+), (-), (+)\):

\(f(x)\) & Derivatives Diagonal Sign \(g(x)\) & Integrals Product Term
\(x^2\) \((+)\) \(e^x\) → \(e^x\) \(+x^2 e^x\)
\(2x\) \((-)\) \(e^x\) → \(e^x\) \(-2x e^x\)
\(2\) \((+)\) \(e^x\) → \(e^x\) \(+2e^x\)
\(0\) (Stop) — \(e^x\) \(= x^2 e^x - 2x e^x + 2e^x + C\)
Worked Example 10.3 (Exam Classic)

Evaluate \(\int x^3 e^{x^2}\,dx\)

Step 1 — Split \(x^3 = x^2 \cdot x\) to prepare for \(e^{x^2}\)
$$\int x^3 e^{x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\int x^2 \left(2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx\right)$$
Step 2 — Apply Integration by Parts with \(u = x^2\) and \(dv = 2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx\)

Then \(du = 2x\,dx\) and \(v = e^{x^2}\):

$$= \frac{1}{2}\left[x^2 e^{x^2} - \int e^{x^2}(2x)\,dx\right]$$
Step 3 — Complete \(\int 2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx = e^{x^2}\)
$$= \frac{1}{2}\left[x^2 e^{x^2} - e^{x^2}\right] + C$$

Differentiation Rules (Part I) + Lecture 6 Methods (Part II)

1. Algebraic, Product, Quotient & Chain Rules

$$\frac{d}{dx}[c] = 0 \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[x^n] = n x^{n-1}$$
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} \quad (\text{Chain Rule})$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}[u \cdot v] = u v' + v u'$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{u}{v}\right] = \frac{v u' - u v'}{v^2}$$

2. Trig, Exponential & Logarithmic Derivatives

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\sin u] = \cos u \cdot u'$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\cos u] = -\sin u \cdot u'$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\tan u] = \sec^2 u \cdot u'$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\sec u] = \sec u \tan u \cdot u'$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\csc u] = -\csc u \cot u \cdot u'$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\cot u] = -\csc^2 u \cdot u'$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}[e^u] = e^u \cdot u'$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}[a^u] = a^u \ln(a) \cdot u'$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}[\ln u] = \frac{1}{u} \cdot u'$$

3. Lecture 6: Separable DEs, L'Hôpital, Growth & Integration by Parts

Separable Differential Equations (§7.4)
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = g(x)h(y) \implies \int \frac{dy}{h(y)} = \int g(x)\,dx$$
L'Hôpital's Rule (§7.5)
$$\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} \quad \left(\frac{0}{0}, \frac{\infty}{\infty}\right)$$
Growth Hierarchy (§7.8)
$$\ln x \ll x^{1/n} \ll x^n \ll b^x \ll e^x$$
Integration by Parts (§8.2)
$$\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du$$
